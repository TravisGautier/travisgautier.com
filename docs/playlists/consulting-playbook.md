# The Consulting Playbook — Playlist Plan

**Series:** Playbook (Tier 2)
**Primary Pillar:** Business — "How do you run this as a business?"
**Crossover Pillars:** Delivery (C4, C9), Workflow (C8)
**Episodes:** 9
**Target Length:** 12–20 min per episode (mechanism episodes shorter, pricing and proposal episodes longer)
**Status:** Planning

---

## Positioning

Every other playlist on this channel answers "can an agent team actually build
this?" This one answers the question that comes before and after: how does the
work reach you, and how do you get paid for it.

That gap is where the audience actually lives. `docs/brand-identity.md` names the
primary audience as freelancers and consultants who are technically curious but
have never delivered custom software, and the secondary audience as developers who
can code but have never scoped, priced, or closed anything. Both groups can watch
the Party Masters series and come away thinking "great, but nobody would hire me
to do that." This playlist is the answer to that sentence.

It is also the playlist with the most verifiable receipts per minute. Every
mechanism here ran on one real engagement: a real market count, a real discovery
call, a real proposal, a real invoice, a real feedback sprint. Nothing in this
playlist is a framework invented for the camera.

### Target Audience

- Freelancers and consultants selling adjacent services (marketing, design, bookkeeping) who want to add software delivery to the offer
- Solo developers who can build but have never scoped, priced, or closed a project
- Agency owners of one who are undercharging because they have no pricing method
- People who have watched the build content and cannot see the path from "I can do this" to "someone paid me to do this"

### What This Playlist Is NOT

- Not a sales-guru playlist. No scripts for manufacturing urgency, no closing tricks, no "high ticket" theater.
- Not a freelancing-platform playlist. Nothing here is about winning a race to the bottom on a bidding site.
- Not generic business advice. Every episode is a mechanism that ran on one engagement, shown as the artifact it produced.
- Not a template dump. Templates are shown working; the reasoning is the episode.

### Pedagogical Structure

The playlist runs in the order the business actually runs: find the work, learn
the business, price and close, run the engagement. A viewer can enter at any act,
but the sequence is the argument. Most people teaching this start at the proposal,
which is the middle. The reason they start there is that finding the work is the
hard part and it is the part nobody has receipts for.

C1 is the deliberate correction. It is the first episode because "how do I find a
client" is the first question, and because the Party Masters engagement genuinely
started with a market count rather than a pitch.

### C1 — Title pending Travis's ruling

**Working titles (proposed, pick one or write a better one):**
- "How I Found a Client by Counting a Market Nobody Was Watching"
- "There Are 3,000 Businesses in This Niche and 2 Competitors"
- "Nobody Teaches the First Step: Finding the Client"

**Runtime target:** 15–18 min
**Pillar:** Business
**Search intent:** "how to find consulting clients," "how to find a niche," "freelance client acquisition"
**Status:** New episode, not in the original 8-title list in `docs/content-architecture.md`. Added because the playlist otherwise begins after the client already exists.

#### Thesis
Market research is the cheapest part of an engagement and the part everyone
skips. You do not find a client by pitching harder. You find one by counting a
market carefully enough to know which businesses are underserved, then walking in
already knowing their problem better than the incumbent does.

#### Outline
1. **Cold open** (60–90s) — The competitive research folder on screen. Three analyses, none written by a human. "This is what I did before I talked to anyone. It took an afternoon."
2. **The wrong question** (2–3 min) — "How do I get clients" is the question everyone asks, and it has no answer because it skips a step. The answer-shaped question is "which businesses have a problem worth $30K and nobody selling them a solution."
3. **Counting a niche** (4–5 min) — The actual method, on screen:
   - Pick an intersection, not a category. Not "DJs." Not "rental companies." Businesses that do both, whose software has to handle two models at once.
   - Count it. Get to a real number of addressable businesses, not a market-size press release.
   - Count the competitors. Two dedicated competitors against 3,000 businesses is the finding, and the finding is the opportunity.
4. **Auditing the incumbents** (4–5 min) — Agent-run teardowns of what those businesses use today. Architecture age, missing capabilities, the gap between advertised and real pricing. Show the eWebmin and DJ Intelligence analyses on screen. The point is not that the incumbents are bad. The point is that the mismatch is specific and nameable, and naming it is what makes you credible on the first call.
5. **The three readings of "local business"** (3–4 min) — This method is not specific to one vertical. Show it working three ways: a local service business, a retail operation, any owner-operator who signs their own checks. What changes is the count. What does not change is the method.
6. **What it cost** (2 min) — The honest part. The research was run by agents and reviewed by one person in an afternoon. That is the whole reason this is available to a one-person shop and was not available five years ago.
7. **Close** (60s) — "I did not find this client by being good at sales. I found them by being the only person who had counted."

### C2 — "Why I Charge for Discovery (And How I Sell It)"

**Runtime target:** 14–16 min
**Pillar:** Business
**Search intent:** "paid discovery," "charge for scoping," "consulting discovery phase"

#### Thesis
A free scoping call trains the client to treat your thinking as free. A paid
discovery phase filters for clients who take their own workflows seriously, and
it produces a deliverable that is genuinely worth the money even if they never
hire you for the build.

#### Outline
1. **Cold open** (60s) — The line from the real call: the discovery phase produces complete technical specifications, a valuable deliverable even if you do not proceed, and it is credited toward development if you do.
2. **Why free scoping fails** (3–4 min) — You do the work, they get the spec, they shop it. Or worse, they never decide, because nothing was ever at stake for them.
3. **What the phase actually delivers** (4–5 min) — Show the toolkit: the folder, the templates, the 15-day plan, the current-state and future-state workflow maps. This is a product, not a meeting.
4. **How to price it** (2–3 min) — The $8-12K framing and where the number comes from: a percentage of the build it de-risks, not an hourly estimate of the calls.
5. **The credit mechanic** (2 min) — Credited toward development removes the "am I paying twice" objection without discounting the work.
6. **The filter** (2 min) — Clients who will not pay for discovery were never going to pay for the build. This is the cheapest disqualification you will ever run.
7. **Close** (60s) — "I sold the phase before I ran it. The framing did a job: it filtered for a client who takes their own workflows seriously."

### C3 — "How to Run a Discovery Call That Closes $30K Projects"

**Runtime target:** 18–20 min
**Pillar:** Business
**Search intent:** "discovery call questions," "client kickoff call," "requirements gathering call"

#### Thesis
A discovery call is not a sales call with questions in it. It is a structured
interview with a prioritization framework, and the preparation is the whole
difference between 13 categories of requirements and a page of notes.

#### Outline
1. **Cold open** (60–90s) — The call strategy guide on screen. "I did not walk into this call and wing it. An agent team built the prep in an afternoon and I reviewed it."
2. **The prep** (4–5 min) — What existed before the call: a call strategy guide, question prioritization, interview templates, a business profile template, a pain-points matrix. Each one shown.
3. **Structure of the call** (5–6 min) — Opening, technical discussion, the natural pivot to investment, the investment discussion. Show why the order matters: you do not price anything before you understand the workflow, because the price is a function of the workflow.
4. **Getting to priorities in their order** (3–4 min) — The five critical priorities in the client's own ranking: calendar, Wix integration, lead-to-booking workflow, quote automation, payment processing. The trick is not asking "what do you want." It is asking them to rank.
5. **Objection handling** (3–4 min) — The four real objections and the four real answers, from the guide. Not scripts. Positions.
6. **Red flags** (2 min) — What ends a call. The guide has a list; read it honestly.
7. **Close** (60s) — "One conversation produced 13 categories of requirements. That is not charisma. That is preparation."

#### Key visuals
- Call strategy guide, question prioritization framework
- The 13 requirement categories as a board
- The five client priorities in the client's own order
- Objection and response cards

### C4 — "How to Audit a Client's Current System Before You Replace It"

**Runtime target:** 14–16 min
**Pillar:** Delivery
**Search intent:** "current state analysis," "system audit consulting," "workflow mapping client"

#### Thesis
You cannot replace a system you have not mapped. The current-state audit is what
turns "they want a CRM" into a specification, and it is also the document that
protects you when the client remembers a workflow nobody mentioned.

#### Outline
1. **Cold open** (60s) — The current-state workflow map on screen next to the future-state map.
2. **What an audit covers** (4–5 min) — Every workflow they run today, timed. Every tool in the stack. Every handoff between people. Every place data is retyped.
3. **The findings that mattered** (4–5 min) — Manual quotes taking 20-30 minutes each. Double-booking risk with no conflict detection. No integration with the website that generated the leads. Each of these is a line item in the eventual proposal and a number in the eventual ROI case.
4. **Current state to future state** (3–4 min) — Show the two maps side by side. The delta is the scope. This is the single most useful artifact in the engagement.
5. **The audit as protection** (2 min) — Written, agreed, dated. When a workflow surfaces in month three that nobody mentioned, the audit is what makes that a change order rather than an argument.
6. **Close** (60s) — "The audit is not paperwork. It is the difference between a spec and a wish."

- The pain-points matrix
- A timed workflow: 20-30 minutes per manual quote, on a stopwatch

### C5 — "How I Price Custom Software: Real Numbers, Real Project"

**Runtime target:** 18–20 min
**Pillar:** Business
**Search intent:** "how to price custom software," "freelance developer rates," "software project pricing"

> **Note:** the ruled title in `docs/content-architecture.md` uses an em dash.
> The 2026-09-06 voice ruling bans em dashes in outbound text, and video titles
> are outbound. Colon form used here; Travis to ratify.

#### Thesis
Price the outcome against the alternative the client already has, then show your
arithmetic. An hourly rate that you can defend against market data is not a
weakness. It is the thing that makes the total believable.

#### Outline
1. **Cold open** (60–90s) — The rate justification card from the real proposal.
2. **The two anchors** (3–4 min) — Every custom software price sits between two numbers the client already knows: what a SaaS subscription costs them over five years, and what an agency would quote. Name both before you name yours.
3. **Defending the rate** (4–5 min) — $100/hour against senior full-stack market rates and agency rates, with the sources cited in the proposal itself. Show the discount and say why it exists: long-term engagement, anchor customer.
4. **Defending the hours** (4–5 min) — 345 hours against an industry benchmark of 300-500 hours for work of this shape. Per-feature breakdowns with an expandable "why this many hours" for each. This is where most quotes die, because the total arrives with no arithmetic behind it.
5. **Investment, not cost** (2–3 min) — The five-year economics framing. Custom loses on month one and wins on month thirty. Show the crossover.
6. **What I will not show you** (1–2 min) — The internal tier sheet is marked not for client distribution and stays that way. State the limit flat and move on.
7. **Close** (60s) — "$34,500. 345 hours at $100. Every line of that arithmetic was on the page before the client asked."

### C6 — "Writing Proposals That Close: The Dual-Path Strategy"

**Runtime target:** 16–18 min
**Pillar:** Business
**Search intent:** "software proposal template," "consulting proposal," "how to write a proposal that wins"

#### Thesis
A single-price proposal is a yes-or-no question. A dual-path proposal is a which
question, and which questions close. Build it as an interactive document rather
than slides, because the client will read it alone, after the call, without you
there to narrate.

#### Outline
1. **Cold open** (60–90s) — The interactive HTML proposal on screen, tabs and all. "This is not a deck. Nobody is presenting this."
2. **Two documents, two jobs** (3–4 min) — The main proposal and the investment overview. One explains the work, one explains the money. Separating them lets the client share the money document internally without shipping your whole methodology.
3. **The dual path** (4–5 min) — The build path and the larger path, priced separately, presented as a choice between two futures rather than an accept-or-decline on one. Show both.
4. **Why HTML and not slides** (3–4 min) — It gets read asynchronously. It expands where the client is curious and stays collapsed where they are not. The per-feature justifications live behind disclosure so the document is short and deep at the same time.
5. **What the document must contain** (3 min) — Scope, arithmetic, timeline, payment structure, what is not included. That last one closes more deals than any of the others.
6. **Close** (60s) — "The proposal is the only part of the engagement the client reads without you in the room. Build it for that."

- Dual-path comparison view

### C7 — "The Milestone Payment Structure That Protects Both Sides"

**Runtime target:** 12–14 min
**Pillar:** Business
**Search intent:** "milestone payments freelance," "project payment schedule," "deposit structure consulting"

#### Thesis
Payment structure is risk allocation, and a good one is legible to both parties in
one sentence. Four equal milestones tied to delivered scope means neither side is
ever more than a quarter exposed.

#### Outline
1. **Cold open** (60s) — The structure on one card: 25% at kickoff, 25% at core completion, 25% at advanced features, 25% at launch.
2. **What each side is afraid of** (3–4 min) — The client is afraid of paying for vapor. You are afraid of building for free. Name both out loud; the structure is the answer to both.
3. **Tying milestones to scope, not dates** (3–4 min) — A date-based schedule pays for elapsed time. A scope-based schedule pays for delivered work, which is what both sides actually want to be true.
4. **The alternatives and when to use them** (3–4 min) — Monthly retainer for predictable cash flow. Phased build when budget is the constraint. Hybrid when the client wants skin in the game. Each with the situation that calls for it.
5. **The deposit conversation** (2 min) — How to ask, and why the ask is a filter rather than a favor.
6. **Close** (60s) — "Neither side is ever more than a quarter exposed. That sentence has closed more than any pitch I have written."

- Scope-tied vs date-tied schedule comparison

### C8 — "The Folder Structure That Runs My Entire Consulting Business"

**Runtime target:** 14–16 min
**Pillar:** Workflow
**Search intent:** "consulting project organization," "freelance file structure," "client project folder"

#### Thesis
Process beats talent, and a folder structure is process you can see. The agent
team works because the context is organized, and the context is organized because
the folders are. This is the least glamorous episode on the channel and the most
reusable.

#### Outline
1. **Cold open** (60s) — The full tree expanding on screen.
2. **The discovery toolkit** (4–5 min) — 12 subfolders, each with a job. Built once by the agent team, reusable on the next engagement. That reusability is the point of building it at all.
3. **Why AI makes structure matter more, not less** (3–4 min) — An agent's output quality is a function of the context it can find. Disorganized context produces confident wrong answers. The folder structure is a prompt you write once.
4. **Templates as compounding assets** (3–4 min) — Business profile, pain points matrix, workflow maps, interview questions. Every engagement improves them; nothing is rebuilt.
5. **What travels between clients and what does not** (2–3 min) — Method travels. Client data never does. Draw the line explicitly.
6. **Close** (60s) — "The agents execute. The process ensures quality. Anyone can learn process."

### C9 — "Handling Client Feedback Like a Pro: 25 Items in One Sprint"

**Runtime target:** 16–18 min
**Pillar:** Delivery
**Search intent:** "client feedback management," "change requests freelance," "feedback triage"

> **Note:** the ruled title in `docs/content-architecture.md` uses an em dash.
> Colon form used here for the same reason as C5; Travis to ratify.

#### Thesis
Feedback is not an interruption to the project. It is the project telling you what
it actually is. Triage it in writing, ship what you agreed to ship, and say out
loud what you are not doing and why.

#### Outline
1. **Cold open** (60–90s) — The action report on screen. 25 items in, 21 built in one sprint.
2. **Receiving feedback without flinching** (3–4 min) — The instinct is to defend. The professional move is to log, categorize, and come back with a plan.
3. **The triage** (4–5 min) — Every item categorized: bug, scope, change, misunderstanding. The categories decide who pays. Show the real report.
4. **21 of 25** (3–4 min) — What shipped in the sprint, and the honest accounting of the four that did not, with the reason for each. The four are the credibility.
5. **Turning feedback into the next milestone** (2–3 min) — A well-run feedback round is where the follow-on engagement comes from.
6. **The agent team's role** (2 min) — Triage is a human judgment call. Implementation is not. Show the split honestly.
7. **Close** (60s) — "25 items in. 21 out in one sprint. The four I did not build are on the report with reasons, and that page is why they trusted the other 21."
